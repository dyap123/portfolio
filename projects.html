    01 LIVE THE HUB

    Command Center — the whole jobsite on one screen.

    The operating picture for a concrete team on a $2B convention center expansion. Schedule, pours, embeds, RFIs, submittals, inspections, tools and crew — behind one role-aware login, on a map generated from the structural model rather than traced from a drawing of it. Five field apps became one platform. It is the screen the superintendent runs the 6 AM tailgate from.

    Problem

    The suite working was the problem. Each app answered its own question well, but the superintendent’s real question crossed four of them at once — what is ready for inspection, what is holding up tomorrow’s pour, who is on site today. Answering it meant four logins, four maps and four versions of the truth. And the plan underneath all of them had been traced by hand off a PDF: shapes with no dimensions, no volumes, and no way to prove any of it matched the structure actually being built.

    Approach

    One platform, one sign-in, one record. The map is generated from the project’s own structural model, so every shape on it is a real member carrying its real dimensions instead of an outline somebody drew over a drawing. Progress is tracked against those members, stage by stage, which means the map, the dashboard and the printed report cannot disagree — they are all reading the same thing. Access is role-based and enforced on the server rather than hidden in the interface, so view-only is what a phone in the field gets by default.

    The way in

    A tool people are told to use gets opened once. So the sign-in is a terminal that greets you by time of day, and the mark above it is a merlion, drawn once as geometry and reused everywhere the app needs a logo. It took five rejections to find the rule underneath it: repetition plus regularity reads as decoration, not as an animal. A flower, a sun and a star are the same construction, so restyling the petal was never going to fix it. Every change that stuck was structural, and three of them were deleting a feature rather than redrawing it.

    Result

    Ten views behind one login, and six separate apps retired into one. The number that matters is not on the screen. It is that the argument about what actually got built — the one that used to happen in the field on pour morning, at the worst possible moment — now happens weeks earlier, on a screen, with everyone looking at the same picture.

    What it is not

    It covers the foundation scope, not the whole structure. It is an internal tool behind a company sign-in, so there is no public demo — the screenshots here are the tour, and the project data on them is redacted. And moving the last of the older apps onto the platform is still in progress.

    02 LIVE

    YapGrid — the foundation, modelled, with the rebar in it.

    A browser 3D model of the LACC foundation, built from the project’s own Revit IFC export. Concrete and formwork taken off the model instead of counted by hand off PDFs. And grade beam bars were running straight through piles. They are not now.

    Problem

    The reinforcing is not in the model. It is not in the IFC either — the export carries the concrete and not one reinforcing bar, because the bars only ever existed as schedule rows and typical details on paper. So quantities got counted by hand off PDFs, and nothing anywhere could answer the question that actually costs money on a pour day: is a bar and a pile trying to occupy the same space?

    Approach

    Three sources, kept separate on purpose. The IFC says where the concrete is and what shape. The schedules say what steel goes in it. The details say how that steel is arranged and terminated — and that third one is in neither of the first two. A grade beam bar running through to the far face of a pile cap and hooking there is a sentence on a typical detail, nowhere else.

    Geometry is imported analytically wherever it can be. 99% of the solids are vertical extrusions of a planar profile, so they come across as real outlines with real voids rather than triangle soup. That is what keeps plan projection, dimensioning, quantity takeoff and clash checking working — all four need a polygon, not a mesh. Only the genuinely non-prismatic remainder gets tessellated.

    Does it agree with the drawings?

    The check that matters is against a source the model never saw. Modelled volumes were compared with the dimensions the schedules themselves print, and they agree to within 0.1% on every dominant mark. When a design addendum landed, all six foundation schedules were re-checked row by row against the model and came back unchanged — what the addendum actually brought was a schedule that had never been issued before, for members that had carried no reinforcing at all until then.

    Client and project data redacted.

    Result

    Quantities you can order from — concrete, formwork contact area, and a bar schedule by size — rolled up by mark rather than one row per element. Enlarged plans generate onto the real title block with dimension chains referenced to the structural grid. And the coordination catch: grade beam bars were passing straight through piles. They now step over them, and where there genuinely is not room, the model says so instead of quietly moving the steel.

    What it is not

    The reinforcing is a typical-detail interpretation, not a placing drawing, and the model says so on every sheet it prints. It covers the foundation levels, not the structure above. Where the imported geometry and a schedule disagree, it refuses to generate rather than guess, and lists the marks. Every one of those caveats is in the app, not just here.

    Real-time concrete-pour platform for the LACC project. Pour plans, embed verification, live trucks, mix designs, inspection ticker, and a camera-driven bulk PDF scanner — all synced across every device in the field.

    Problem

    On a pour day you have 50+ moving parts: concrete trucks arriving, rebar placement status, embeds to verify, inspectors to coordinate, and a PM chewing through a printed footing map. The existing tools were: a spreadsheet, a clipboard, and walkie-talkies.

    Approach

Foremen drag footing shapes on the map, long-press embeds to step through a checklist, tap a truck arriving to log it live, and a new bulk scanner uses the camera + edge detection to ingest multi-page ticket PDFs.

    04 LIVE

    EmbedYap — 299 embeds, zero guesswork.

    Every anchor bolt and knife plate in the foundation, extracted from the plan PDFs with computer vision and tracked as live pins on a pan/zoom map. Miss an embed before a pour and you're coring concrete; EmbedYap exists so that never happens.

    Problem

    The embed plan was a PDF, a highlighter, and somebody's memory. 299 embeds across 75 types had to be installed in the right footing before each pour — and a missed one isn't a redo, it's a core drill through cured structural concrete with an engineer's sign-off. Status lived in whoever walked the deck last.

    Build

    A Python + OpenCV pipeline reads the structural plan PDFs — classifying the orange install dots and OCR-ing the red type labels — and seeds Firebase with 299 georeferenced pins across 75 embed types. The front end renders them on a pan/zoom canvas map: green installed, red to-install, amber next in sequence, blue knife plates. Originally React; I later ported it to a tiny vanilla-JS runtime — same UX, no framework, no build step, faster on trailer iPads.

    05 LIVE

    Tool Tracker — the conex stopped lying.

    An Assets-as-truth equipment registry for the field: orders, receiving, per-foreman checklists, a lost-tool log, and on-site verification. The workflow it replaced — legacy-system exports stapled to printed count sheets — lost roughly 65 tools in a single cycle.

    Problem

    Tool accountability ran on printouts taped to the trailer wall: an export from the legacy asset system, re-keyed by hand, out of date the moment it printed. Tools moved between the conex, the deck, and foremen's trucks all day; the paper never followed. Across the previous cycle, about 65 tools simply disappeared — replacement cost plus the PM hours spent hunting them.

    Build

    A Firebase-backed registry where the Assets list is the single source of truth. Legacy CSV/PDF exports reconcile against it with alias resolution (the same drill has four names in four systems); assignments log who has what; a "verified on site" checklist catches missing or mis-tagged tools before they walk. The same data drives a printable bilingual 11×17 count poster for the trailer wall — print catching up to digital instead of fighting it.

    07 FLAGSHIP

    Mr. George — a project-engineer assistant on every channel I work in.

    Internally codenamed Mr. George. Seventeen AI agents that read my emails, write my submittals, search 200+ drawings, dispatch to a research panel, and page me on Discord when something breaks. The const-agent web UI is the cockpit.

    Problem

    A project engineer on a $2B job spends most of their day shuffling: copying RFI responses into trackers, searching drawings for one detail, drafting submittal cover letters, chasing down inspector confirmations. LLMs are good at every one of these tasks individually. Nobody had wired them together for construction PM yet.

    Approach

    Built a multi-channel agent framework in Python. The same 17 agents — submittal coordinator, RFI triager, schedule analyst, drawing indexer, takeoff specialist, tool tracker, foreman roster, advisory panel of five — answer in Discord, Teams, and a dedicated web UI. Each channel routes to the right agent based on the message.

    Model providers are hot-swappable: Anthropic (Claude Opus), OpenAI (Codex via ChatGPT OAuth), MiniMax M2.7, Gemini, Ollama for local. If one goes down, the next provider picks up. Vision is enabled — paste an image directly into Alfred and he'll read it.

    Formwork — a budget tool built around the way construction teams already think.

    OpenYap's own budget tracker. Categories sit inside formwork bays; planned spend reads as concrete pouring into each section; line items show as studs in the form. Underneath the metaphor: a Sankey flow view, a Cmd-K palette, a health gauge for variance against plan, and a 3D lattice for the compare view.

    Problem

    Running a venture means tracking every dollar of the $1.2M seed across LLC / legal / infra / dev / marketing / ops. Spreadsheets work but don't let you think in line items inside each budget, and they don't show the flow of money. I wanted a construction-native UX for money.

    Approach

    Firebase-backed single-file app. Each category drills into line items with statuses (planned / committed / done). The Plan view toggles between a Sankey flow and a Grid; scenarios let you fork the budget for what-ifs; Cmd-K palette hops to anything; a health gauge surfaces variance against plan in one glance. The Nodes view is a 3D formwork lattice — wireframe bays sized by planned total, concrete fill ∝ actual spend.

    OpenYap Roadmap — the investor deck as a building.

    A scroll-driven 3D construction site that reads as the OpenYap pitch deck. Each section of the page builds a floor of the tower; a lattice crane climbs alongside the scroll position. We deploy two themes so we can match the audience.

    Problem

    A five-phase business plan delivered as slides loses people on slide three. We wanted a version of the same content that an investor or board member could absorb in roughly ninety seconds without giving it their full attention.

    Approach
