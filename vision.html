Sheet A-301 · Vision — Why I Build ISSUED FOR REVIEW
Why I build

I build things people actually use.

A wooden robot, a soldering iron, a race car, a five-app suite on a jobsite. The through-line has never changed: find someone whose day is harder than it needs to be, and build until it isn't.

Impact 01 · The crew

The people the software industry forgot.

Construction crews are some of the least-served users in software — most tools they're handed were designed for an office they never sit in. Every OpenYap app is built the other way around: operable with one gloved thumb, in the sun, at 6 AM. And because translation is unpaid work a foreman absorbs, every sheet ships bilingual EN/ES from day one.

  • Foreman-first design — gloves on, sun in your eyes
  • Bilingual EN/ES standard, not an afterthought
  • Print and digital in sync — the trailer wall counts too
  • Tools people adopt without being told to
Impact 02 · The team

A day a week, given back.

The suite saves each project engineer about a day a week of re-keying, hunting, and formatting. That's not an efficiency metric to me — it's afternoons that go to actual engineering, mentoring, or getting home before dark. Good software's most honest impact is measured in someone else's hours.

  • ~1 day/week back per PE, field-measured
  • RFI prep 40 → 6 minutes
  • The weekly look-ahead: an afternoon → minutes
  • $75K/yr in vendor costs avoided through careful evaluation
Impact 03 · The next generation

Paying the wooden robot forward.

I exist as a builder because a small-town robotics team let a kid hand-cut plywood into a competition robot. So I taught K–12 robotics and STEM at STEM Center USA through early 2026 — same loop, smaller hands: give someone a real problem, real tools, and the confidence that they're allowed to build the fix themselves.

  • K–12 robotics & STEM instruction · STEM Center USA
  • FIRST Robotics roots — CowTech FRC alum
  • Teaching the loop: problem → build → test → again
  • The best tool I can hand a kid is permission to build
Impact 04 · The industry

Prove the field deserves great software.

Construction builds the physical world and runs on some of the oldest workflows in it. I want my career — wherever it goes — to keep proving that field-first, AI-native software makes jobs safer, faster, and more humane. The playbook I'm building on one $2B jobsite is a playbook for every under-served industry that works with its hands.

  • Field-first AI as the default, not the demo
  • Safety and quality rise when status is visible
  • One jobsite proven → any jobsite possible
  • Hands-on industries deserve first-class tools
Process

How I build.

Six steps, one loop. Field first, polish last — then do it again.

01

Work hands-on in the field.

Find what really needs improving. If a foreman can't use it with one hand and no English, it doesn't work.

02

Design around what we have.

Design the framework from our existing systems — whatever makes the most sense.

03

Build a core function.

Create the one core function, then test it in the field.

04

Test in the field. Fix bugs.

See if it works on site. Fix what breaks.

05

Make it sharp.

Build a cool, modern front end and add the features that make it shine.

06

Repeat and scale.

Then do it all again — and scale.

Why now

AI is only just becoming AI.

For most of the last few years, "AI" meant a large language model doing very confident autocomplete — pattern-matching its way to an answer and hoping it landed. That era is ending. The frontier is moving from models that retrieve to models that reason, and we're standing at the very front of that curve.

Until now

Autocomplete in a trench coat

Base LLMs predict the next token. Impressive, but brittle — they can't tell you when they're wrong, and they fold on anything that needs real multi-step thought. Most "AI features" shipping today are still just this.

trending_flat
Starting now

Models that actually reason

A new class — trained with reinforcement learning to think before they answer, spend compute at inference time, plan, check their own work, and back up when they're wrong. o1/o3, DeepSeek-R1, extended-thinking Claude: the first generation that reasons instead of recites.

This is the inflection — slowly but surely, AI is crossing from a party trick into something that can carry real weight on a real project. Most of the industry is still bolting last year's autocomplete onto a dashboard. I've spent the journey on the other path: in the field every day, grounded in the actual machine learning instead of just the API, and shipping reasoning-driven tools that already hold up on a $2B job. That's why the time is now — and why I'm all-in on building for the people the software industry forgot.
